# AI Lesson - Teachers Notes

**Lesson aim**

- Build student habits for clear prompting, spotting errors/hallucinations, and verifying information from AI tools.

**Timing overview (60 minutes)**

- Intro and objectives: 5 min
- AI/LLM basics + advantages/problems: 12 min
- Prompt generation practice: 8–10 min
- Hallucinations + verification: 7–8 min
- Practical accuracy exercise (group): 20 min
- Debrief + norms + exit: 5–8 min

**Slide-by-slide teacher guidance and scripts**

1) Title: Using AI Wisely in School
- Script: “Today we’re learning how to use AI to learn better—not to skip learning. We’ll practice writing clear prompts and verifying AI answers.”
- Quick check: “Who’s used AI in the past month? For what?”

2) Objectives
- Script: “By the end, you can explain what LLMs do, write a strong prompt, spot typical AI errors, and verify key claims with sources.”

3) AI/LLM: what it is (simple, non-technical)
- Talking points:
  - LLMs predict the next likely words. They generate plausible text; they don’t automatically “know” truth.
  - Great for drafting and explaining; still needs human verification.
- Quick demo idea (optional): Ask an AI, “In 2 sentences, explain how a refrigerator keeps food cold for a 15-year-old, include one misconception people have.” Use it to discuss clarity vs. certainty.

4) Where we see AI (subject-neutral)
- Examples:
  - School: study notes, summaries, practice questions, translation.
  - Everyday: recommendations (music/video), autocomplete, voice assistants.
- Prompt students: “Share one helpful use and one risky use.”

5) Advantages vs. problems (T-chart)
- Advantages examples:
  - Speed: outline an essay/report.
  - Personalised explanations at your level.
  - Drafting question banks or revision checklists.
- Problems examples:
  - Hallucinated (invented) citations or facts.
  - Privacy/data concerns if you paste sensitive info.
  - Over-reliance: losing practice in reading/thinking.
- Script: “Use AI to think with, not to think for you.”

6) Prompting: weak vs strong (universal template)
- Weak: “Tell me about climate change.”
- Strong: “Explain two key drivers of climate change for a 15-year-old in ≤120 words. Include 1 everyday example and 1 common misconception. Give 2 sources with author, date, and working links.”
- Checklist (display or say):
  - Audience and purpose
  - Format and length (bullets vs paragraph)
  - Number of examples or misconceptions
  - Timeframe or context (country, years)
  - Sources with titles, authors, dates, and working links
- Quick pair task (2–3 min): Rewrite a weak prompt into a strong one on any subject they pick.

7) Hallucinations and red flags (what to watch for)
- Red flags to say out loud:
  - Confident tone, no sources
  - Fake-looking citations or broken links
  - Specific numbers without references
  - Misattributed quotes or mixed-up terms (e.g., correlation vs causation)
- Script: “Confidence isn’t correctness. Treat unsourced claims like rumors until verified.”

8) Verification techniques (teacher-friendly, any subject)
- Three steps:
  1) Require sources (titles, authors, dates, links).
  2) Cross-check with a trusted site or textbook.
  3) Ask AI to self-audit: “List uncertainties; show how each source supports claim X.”
- Methods to model:
  - Site search: site:who.int measles vaccine or site:britannica.com Versailles
  - Check dates and authors; skim for alignment (does the page actually say the claim?)
  - Use Wayback Machine for dead links.

9) Practical exercise: Information accuracy challenge
- Instructions (on slide/board):
  - Choose a focused, checkable question.
  - Prompt AI: “Give 3–5 bullet claims. For each, include source title, author/organization, date, and working link.”
  - Record claims + links, then verify each with a second source or site search.
  - Summarize what’s verified, uncertain, or wrong.
- Grouping: pairs or triads. Set a 12–15 minute timer.

**Subject-neutral question bank (pick any)**

- Health: “List two evidence-based impacts of teen sleep deprivation on learning (2020–2025 sources).”
- History: “Name three terms of the 1919 Treaty of Versailles with official/primary sources.”
- Science: “Describe two greenhouse gases and one key source of each (recent sources).”
- Geography: “Identify two causes and one consequence of coastal erosion in the UK or Ireland (recent sources).”
- Media literacy: “Give three signs a news article is credible, with examples.”
- Computing/media: “List two benefits and two risks of facial recognition in public spaces with policy sources.”

**Model prompts students can copy**

- “Provide 3–5 bullet-point claims answering: [your focused question]. For each claim, give the source’s title, author/organization, publication date, and a working link. If uncertain, state uncertainty.”
- Follow-up check: “Explain in one sentence how each source supports its claim. If any link is broken or off-topic, replace it.”

10) Monitor during exercise: teacher moves
- If prompts are vague: “Who’s the audience? How many bullets? Ask for dates and working links.”
- If links are broken: “Try site search: site:domain keyword; if still missing, mark as unverified.”
- If two sources disagree: “Note the disagreement; prefer official/primary or consensus bodies; record uncertainty.”

11) Debrief (share 3–4 quick examples)
- Ask:
  - “Which prompt constraint helped most?”
  - “One claim you verified and the source?”
  - “One error/uncertainty you caught and how?”
- Takeaways to say:
  - “Specific prompts produce checkable answers.”
  - “Verification is non-negotiable, even when it sounds right.”
  - “Transparency: note when AI helped and how you checked it.”

12) Class norms and exit
- Post norms:
  - Be transparent (cite AI use).
  - Verify (check facts/links).
  - Learn first (try before asking AI).
  - Privacy (no personal/sensitive data).
  - Originality (you must understand your work).
- Exit ticket (2 minutes):
  - “Write one strong prompt you’ll use next time.”
  - “Note one correction or improvement from today.”

**Ready-to-read mini scripts you can lift**

- Introducing LLMs:
  - “Think of this as supercharged autocomplete. It’s great at producing plausible text, but it doesn’t guarantee the facts are correct. That’s why we verify.”
- Upgrading a prompt:
  - “Add audience, format, word limit, and sources with dates. For example: ‘Explain X for a 15-year-old in ≤120 words, include 1 analogy and 2 examples, and cite 2 recent sources with working links.’”
- Spotting red flags:
  - “Confident answer, no sources? That’s a red flag. Ask for author, date, and a working link for each claim.”
- Verification nudge:
  - “Click the link. Does the page actually say that? If not, mark it uncertain and find a second source.”

**Differentiation and extensions**

- Support:
  - Provide a prompt frame students can fill: audience, length, 3–5 bullets, sources with dates and links.
  - Offer a list of reputable domains per subject if classes vary.
- Extension:
  - Compare two AI tools’ answers; decide which is better evidenced and why.
  - Build a simple credibility rubric: author, date, domain, evidence quality (score 1–3 each).

**Assessment cues**

- Look for:
  - Clear, constrained prompts.
  - Verification logs with at least one confirmed source per claim.
  - Final summaries distinguishing verified vs. uncertain.
- Ask:
  - “How did the second source confirm or challenge the claim?”
  - “What uncertainty did you leave in, and why?”
